Comparative information: ASUIR Service (a service generator) versus ASUIR (a generator of ultra-efficient production facilities).

As with ASUIR, hospitals, laboratories and hotels can be viewed as production facilities that generate services, such as procedures, tests or X-rays, rather than goods. In this sense, the principles of ASUIR and ASUIR Service are similar: staff salaries are at US levels or higher, while the cost of the service (in a hospital, this is a procedure) is comparable to that in Bangladesh — one of the cheapest countries in the world, where staff salaries are similar to those in the US (here, $10,000).

At the same time, the ASUIR service's increased productivity is not achieved by increasing the workload of service personnel, but by automating production through the use of robotic systems.

Automation Step by Step: From Manual Service Production to Production Largely Carried Out by Robotic Devices.

1. People: Everything is done by people.
2. People + ready-made devices The purchase of certain devices that are already in use in hospitals will result in a reduction in the number of support staff. These devices include electronic queuing systems, self-service kiosks where patients can measure their own blood pressure and temperature, and IV pumps controlled by a central console.
3. Future: robotic systems Robotic devices will operate in two shifts, seven days a week. This will be possible once approval is granted for robots to be used in patient care.

The effectiveness of a given option is assessed in the same way as for an automated control system in a power plant. The hospital's equipment, building and employees' salaries, including the doctor's, are considered direct costs and are factored into the service's cost. The cost of medication is included in the service cost and is the same for all options.

1. Why automation goes in stages

Healthcare is a conservative industry, with new equipment only being introduced after it has been tested and approved. The automation of healthcare services therefore proceeds in three stages:

  • Stage 1 — today. All work is done by people, the staff.
  • Stage 2 — partial automation. Ready-made devices are added that are already sold and used in hospitals.
  • Stage 3 — almost full automation (as seen from today). Service staff are being replaced by robotic devices. However, a certain number of doctors and nurses will still be needed. This will become possible once robotic systems are approved for use with patients, which is expected to happen in 2031.

2. What is purchased at each stage

Stage 2 — purchase of devices along the lines of the list below:

  • an electronic queue with a display board to call patients;
  • self-check machines ( units) for blood pressure, pulse, oxygen and temperature;
  • IV pumps, one per chair, for automated drug dosing;
  • A single central control panel for each ward displays the status of all IV drips in one place, helping to reduce the number of nurses needed.

Stage 3 — in addition to the Stage 2 devices, robotic systems are purchased. One robotic system is assumed to serve up to four chairs.

For the engineer: how it is calculated

Procedures per year = chairs × hours per day ÷ (procedure + chair preparation) × days per year.

Medical staff = people per shift × (hours per day ÷ 8) × (days per year ÷ 250).

Doctors work during the day (usually until 5 pm), so their number does not change when the unit works longer hours.

Direct costs per year = pay of staff and doctors per year + purchase price of devices and robotic systems ÷ service life + their repair and maintenance + rent of premises.

Required productivity = direct costs per year ÷ working days per year ÷ payment for one procedure.

Full cost of one procedure = drug + direct costs per year ÷ procedures per year.

Break-even purchase price of a robotic system — the price at which the hospital's cost per procedure in option 3 equals option 2.
